What problems do I help with?

Make confusing systems workable

I’m useful when a codebase, workflow, or technical problem has become hard to reason about. I trace what is really happening, find the pressure point, and turn the problem into something a person or team can act on.

Build tools around real constraints

I’ve worked across web, mobile, desktop, and CLI applications for small teams and client workflows. The valuable part is not the format of the tool; it is whether the tool removes a bottleneck, exposes the right information, or gives someone more control over their work.

Keep scope tied to the purpose

I’m not interested in adding features just to make a project feel bigger. I try to find the focused version that proves the value, then expand where the work calls for it.

What is it like working with me?

I start by making the problem legible

I like to slow down enough to understand what is happening in practice: the code path, the workflow, the person using the tool, and the constraint that keeps showing up. That makes the next step less speculative.

I choose the amount of tool the problem deserves

Sometimes the right move is a repair, a script, a dashboard, or a better deployment path. Sometimes it is a new interface. I care about solving the problem without making the surrounding work heavier.

I care about the human texture of the result

My background in music and audio makes me sensitive to timing, friction, feel, and craft. I still want the work to be practical, but I do not think practical work has to feel generic.

Where has this shown up professionally?

Selected contexts include work through Scaling Startups on Peakery, Polka Dot Sky Software, Hydra Solutions, AirLocker, AudioPile, and private-client tooling.

Untangling production problems

In client and startup work, I’ve been brought into systems where the problem was not obvious from the surface: slow database queries, memory leaks, security exposure, brittle deployments, or inherited code that nobody wanted to touch. The value was turning that confusion into a clear fix: faster responses, safer infrastructure, fewer fragile paths, and a team that could move again.

Building around real workflows

Some consulting work has been less about making a product and more about removing a painful piece of someone’s day. That has included desktop and command-line software for image review, text extraction, file processing, reporting, and Windows distribution. The value was practical: fewer manual steps, fewer errors, and a tool shaped around how the work was already happening.

Working under field constraints

I’ve also worked on mobile and infrastructure problems where the software had to hold up outside ideal conditions: offline mode for network-constrained environments, geolocation and NFC asset tracking, sensitive health-related data, HTTPS migrations, Cloudflare-only ingress, and Terraform-managed infrastructure. The value was making the system safer and more dependable without stopping the work around it.

What else have I built?

Far Reach Co. and tabletop tools

Far Reach Co. is a co-owned company I build with partners, and 5e-Bot comes from the same interest in keeping people inside the shared moment. A good tabletop tool reduces the setup tax: players can join by link, GMs can keep maps, dice, characters, assets, and campaign records in one place, and Discord groups can look up rules or track initiative without breaking conversation. The value is not just a feature list; it is protecting the flow of play.

Music, SFX, and audio tools

SF Audio Guild is another co-owned company. Alongside Tape Sim and Far Reach Radio, it circles the same idea: audio should support the feeling of the work without becoming a technical chore. Music and SFX give scenes identity. Tape Sim explores a direct recording workflow for musicians who think with transport controls and takes. Far Reach Radio lets a group hear the same ambience in sync from a simple link, which keeps the mood shared instead of making every player manage their own setup.

Mayura English patch

The Mayura translation is preservation and access work. It helps English-speaking users experience a piece of Japanese early-internet desktop culture without overwriting the original installation or redistributing the author’s work. The value is careful translation, reversible installation, and respect for a niche art object that would otherwise remain difficult for many people to encounter.
