What is in the download?

The current ZIP contains a script-based Windows patcher. A conventional signed installer is planned.

  • Install-Mayura-English.cmd starts the patcher.
  • Install-Mayura-English.ps1 verifies Mayura, preserves save data, and creates the English copy.
  • patch-manifest.json contains the translation patch data and file checksums.
  • README.txt contains installation instructions, permission terms, and credits.

Translation process

Why I translated Mayura.

Mayura reminds me of what I miss about early internet and computer culture: the games, the unusual little desktop apps, and how much personality they could have. She also offers a glimpse into a Japanese side of that era that I’m only now getting to know. There’s something familiar about having a little character on your desktop, talking about games and doing her own thing. Her conversations made me curious about the world behind them.

I am studying the Japanese language, and it’s a tremendous undertaking. My knowledge is still limited, so I used machine translation for the English dialogue, guided its direction, and made sure the conversations were translated in context.

One detail that caught my interest was “Shiteoku,” a phrase that leads back to the magazine culture of the early ’90s. The ALL ABOUT ベーマガ reference site traces its first appearance to the February 1992 issue of Micom BASIC Magazine, in the “Naniwa Tsūshin” column. The examples give it the feeling of a small, oddly specific inconvenience: too petty to be a real disaster, but unpleasant enough to leave you thinking, “Well, I guess that happened.” Readers sent in their own “Shiteoku” experiences and even rated them on a scale of ten. Finding that little shared joke behind Mayura’s dialogue made the culture she came from feel much more tangible to me.

Mayura’s dialogue is full of puns, in-jokes, and references rooted in Japanese gaming and computer culture. Some depend on wordplay or background knowledge that an English translation can’t easily carry across. We may miss the joke as English-speaking readers, and some translated lines may still seem strange or confusing. The translation can also miss a reference or get its meaning wrong.

My hope is to give the people who still love Mayura a better experience in English, with more of her personality and interests coming through, and help new Ukagaka fans discover why she still has a place on people’s desktops all these years later.
